<h2>Project Overview</h2>

<p>This applied research project was completed in 2024 through the UBC Sustainability Scholars Program in partnership with the City of Vancouver. I evaluated the financial, operational, and sustainability implications of transitioning Vancouver Fire Rescue Services (VFRS) from internal combustion engine vehicles to battery electric fire service vehicles.</p>

<p>The work combined lifecycle cost modeling, fleet-performance analysis, and case studies from fire services in Canada and around the world. The goal was to help decision-makers understand where electrification can create long-term value while preserving the reliability required for emergency response.</p>

<h2>My Approach</h2>

<h4>Fleet and Cost Analysis</h4>
<p>I analyzed capital asset records, energy and fuel use, maintenance and repair costs, insurance premiums, call volumes, response times, and vehicle uptime. The comparison considered financial, operational, and sustainability indicators rather than treating acquisition price as the only decision criterion.</p>

<h4>Lifecycle Financial Model</h4>
<p>I developed a Total Cost of Ownership model comparing a battery electric fire truck with an internal combustion engine pumper over a 15-year expected service life. The model incorporated acquisition costs, incentives, charging infrastructure, energy, maintenance, and insurance.</p>

<h4>Case-Study Research</h4>
<p>I reviewed electric fire vehicle deployments across Canadian and international municipalities to identify practical lessons in infrastructure planning, funding, staff training, operational testing, and phased implementation.</p>

<h2>Key Findings</h2>

<p><b>Higher acquisition cost, lower modeled lifetime cost.</b> Under the report's assumptions, the battery electric vehicle's higher upfront investment was offset by lower energy and maintenance costs over its expected service life. The model estimated a total lifetime cost of approximately CAD $2.84 million for the BEV and CAD $3.26 million for the ICE pumper.</p>

<p><b>Energy costs were materially lower and more predictable.</b> Estimated monthly energy cost was approximately CAD $328 for the electric fire truck, compared with approximately CAD $1,360 in fuel cost for the ICE pumper.</p>

<p><b>Operational readiness must remain central.</b> Response-time and uptime data indicated that electric vehicles can be competitive in emergency-service operations, but the electric fire truck had a short operating history. Continued performance monitoring is therefore essential before applying the findings across the fleet.</p>

<h2>From Analysis to Action</h2>

<p>The research recommended a phased transition rather than a one-time fleet replacement:</p>

<p>1. <b>Prioritize high-cost replacements.</b> Begin with older, high-maintenance, low-efficiency vehicles where electrification can deliver the greatest operational savings.</p>
<p>2. <b>Use incentives strategically.</b> Pursue available funding to reduce the acquisition and infrastructure cost premium.</p>
<p>3. <b>Plan charging as critical infrastructure.</b> Coordinate fire-hall electrical capacity, charging equipment, utility requirements, and backup strategies.</p>
<p>4. <b>Prepare people and operations.</b> Pair deployment with staff training, operational testing, and clear performance measures.</p>
<p>5. <b>Continuously refine the model.</b> Update assumptions as more real-world data becomes available on energy use, maintenance, uptime, response times, and battery performance.</p>

<h2>Limitations and Publication Note</h2>

<p>The analysis reflects the data and assumptions available in 2024. The electric fire truck had a limited operating history, matched fleet records were available for only a subset of vehicles, and future costs remain sensitive to energy prices, incentives, technology, and operating conditions.</p>
